Crisis / situation: Ukraine

--- Report 1: UNHCR (2026-07-23) ---
Overview
This report synthesizes five Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI)-based post distribution monitoring (PDM) assessments covering 2,150 households (HHs), examining humanitarian house repair for both war-affected and internally displaced people (IDPs), construction materials and Rapid Thermal Kit (RTK) assistance delivered in 2025. Sources: Windows Replacement (June 2026, n=290), House Repairs (May 2026, n=561), Construction Materials (May 2026, n=537), IDP Housing Repairs (March 2026, n=454), and Rapid Thermal Kits (April–May 2026, n=308). Service providers: Kyiv Institute of Sociology and Info Sapiens. Target sample sizes for each assessment were determined using a standard statistical sample-size formula (the normal-approximation/Cochran method), applying a 99% confidence level against each programme.

--- Report 2: UNHCR (2026-07-23) ---
Context and Overview
▪ Winter 2025-2026 was particularly harsh in Ukraine. A report by the UN HRMMU found that Russian Armed Forces systematically and repeatedly targeted energy facilities across Ukraine throughout the 2025-2026 winter months – with electricity generation, transmission and distribution facilities hit at least 423 times between October 2025 and March 2026, including 48 coordinated attacks that targeted three or more regions using multiple types of weapons. These attacks triggered emergency electricity outages, affecting millions of civilians across the country, and disrupted critical utilities to hundreds of thousands of people, leaving many without heating and hot water as temperatures fell below -20°C in the coldest winter in Ukraine since 2010. The impact of these outages was particularly severe for people living in multistory buildings and for people with reduced mobility, including older persons and persons with disabilities.
▪ The REACH Cold Spots Risk Assessment for the 2026-2027 winter season found that the highest levels of risk remain concentrated in the north-east and east of Ukraine, particularly across Kharkivska, Sumska, Donetska, and Kyivska oblasts, where colder winter conditions overlap with war intensity, proximity to active hostilities or the Russian border, displacement, infrastructure disruption, and cumulative damage to electricity, water, and heating systems. Compared to previous years, winter-related vulnerability is increasingly shaped not only by proximity to the frontline, but also by broader energy-system disruption affecting large urban and densely populated areas. The REACH assessment highlights the growing importance of large urban areas – including cities such as Kyiv, Kharkiv, Odesa, and Dnipro – where disruptions to electricity, heating, water, and communication systems may affect large numbers of people. Meanwhile, parts of Khersonska, Zaporizka, and Odeska oblasts in southern Ukraine also demonstrate winter-related vulnerability linked to war exposure, infrastructure disruption, and utility outages.
▪ UNHCR’s border monitoring found that the lack of basic services also influenced people’s decisions to flee abroad last winter. People leaving Ukraine started citing lack of electricity, water, and heating among the top reasons for leaving in September 2025. In December 2025, January and February 2026, around 75% of people who left mentioned either lack of electricity, water or heating. About one-third of respondents who left Ukraine in January and February 2026 said that stabilization of the electricity situation was a main factor influencing a future return decision.

Spotlight: Ukraine intensifies its campaign of targeting Russian vessels to cripple maritime export and supply routes
Beginning on 6 July, Ukraine has embarked on a campaign of targeted drone strikes on Russian and Russian-affiliated vessels, primarily in the Azov Sea, but also in the Black Sea. By 13 July, the number of impacted vessels exceeded 100 and will likely keep growing until maritime traffic to the Russian and Russian-controlled shores in the Azov Sea is effectively crippled. Most of the targeted vessels were Russian and foreign-flagged shadow fleet oil tankers, but Ukrainian drones also struck several tugboats, cargo ships, and ferries. During one of the attacks on 10 July, a crew member of an oil tanker was killed.
These naval attacks are yet another escalation in Ukraine’s campaign of targeting the Russian oil industry and energy sector more broadly, which started with long-range attacks on mainly export-oriented oil infrastructure in Russia back in 2024. The campaign has since expanded to other fuel and power infrastructure, as well as to Russian shadow fleet vessels transporting sanctioned oil. This latest series of strikes completely overshadowed attacks on over 20 Russian shadow fleet vessels recorded since 2025, prior to this escalation.
The choice of the Azov Sea as the area of focus for these strikes seems aimed not just at clogging traffic but also at being symbolic, since the sea is surrounded by a Russian-controlled coast and is treated by Russia as its internal waters. All vessels in the area have either a Russian registration or a Russian authorization to engage in Russian trade or logistics. The attacks also support Ukraine’s recent campaign to isolate Crimea by targeting not only the land logistics network but also maritime supply routes.
Ukraine justified the campaign by claiming that Russian shadow fleet vessels are lawful military targets because they financially sustain the Russian war in Ukraine due to sanction evasion and high profits. The legality of targeting a civilian industry due to its financial contribution to military spending is heavily disputed, particularly in the naval context, where seizure – not the targeting of enemy civilian vessels – is a recognized lawful method of economic warfare. In particular, the Secretary-General of the International Maritime Organization condemned the attacks against civilian merchant ships in the Azov and Black Seas, stating that they were not parties to the conflict. Aside from the legality of targeting the oil industry, Russian shadow fleet vessels have also been used by Russia for hybrid warfare and intelligence gathering, transporting Russian troops, and sailing under military convoys, though it is not clear whether any of the vessels hit during this campaign engaged in this activity.
As a result of Ukraine’s naval campaign, Russia decided to halt shipping through the Don-Azov canal, an important maritime corridor for exporting oil, agricultural products, and other goods, but also an exit route for the Caspian flotilla of the Russian Navy.
