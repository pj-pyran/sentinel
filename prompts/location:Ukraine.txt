Crisis / situation: Ukraine

--- Report 1: World Vision (2026-06-25) ---
World Vision started working in Ukraine in March 2022 through 6 and now 3 active offices in Ukraine (Kyiv, Dnipro, Mykolaiv), which currently support aid efforts reaching conflict-affected people. While also supported Ukrainian refugees and host communities in Moldova, Romania, and Georgia. WV UCR in Moldova concluded its operations in June 2025 and transitioned to WV Romania registered Moldova Foundation.
World Vision’s Ukraine Crisis Response and its local partners have reached over 2.3 million people, including more than 1 million children, providing essential support through food, non-food items, cash assistance, protection and education interventions, livelihood, and mental health &amp; psychosocial support programming.
In 2025, World Vision intentionally incorporated early recovery and economic development into its programming, expanding livelihood opportunities for affected families and creating new jobs.
Its microfinance arm, VisionFund, is operating in Ukraine in the field of medium- to long-term recovery and development via financing small and medium-sized businesses for further growth, opportunities to generate income and create new jobs.
Ukraine Crisis Response Goal
The Ukrainian children, refugees and IDPs, as well as the host communities in Ukraine, Moldova, Romania, and Georgia have the support to overcome the humanitarian consequences of the war crisis.
UCR Strategic Objectives
1 Early recovery and economic development opportunities are created for IDP and refugee households, as well as host communities for improved and self-reliant livelihood.
2 Children, their families and communities benefit from enhanced social protection mechanisms and education opportunities in Ukraine and host countries.
3 Basic needs of vulnerable children, families and communities affected by the war are met in Ukraine and host countries.
4 Equitable and complementary partnerships with national and local actors are encultured and promoted to enable them take stronger leadership role in delivering the humanitarian response and managing the transition to rehabilitation and development.

Russian forces advanced in the Donetsk region, occupying the settlements of Pryiut, Rozkishne, and Tykhonivka following clashes with Ukrainian forces. In the Sumy region, they also claimed the capture of Myropillia and Zapsillia, both bordering Russia’s Belgorod region.
Russian forces launched at least 33 long-range missile and drone attacks, including on the Kyiv, Odesa, and Zhytomyr regions.
Russian strikes killed at least 51 civilians in the Sumy, Donetsk, Zaporizhia, Kharkiv, Kherson, Dnipropetrovsk, and Mykolaiv regions. Ukrainian drone strikes reportedly killed seven civilians in the Russian-controlled parts of the Luhansk, Zaporizhia, Kherson, and Donetsk regions.

During the week of 6 to 12 June, Russian forces attacked at least 14 petrol stations in Ukraine’s front line regions, in a trend that had been intensifying since April. On 9 June alone, Russian drones damaged three different petrol stations in the villages of Korobochkyne, Malynivka, and Pechenihy in the Kharkiv region. In the Chernihiv region, Russian drones attacked petrol stations in Koriukivka and Novhorod-Siversky. They also attacked the cities of Nikopol and Pavlohrad and the villages of Bohdanivka and Hrushivka in the Dnipropetrovsk region, as well as a petrol station in the city of Kherson. In some of these strikes, Russian forces launched small drones at targets 20 to 25 kilometers within the front line. They mostly caused fires to erupt, without inflicting mass casualties. This suggests that the aim of these attacks was to impede civilian access to fuel rather than achieve any military objectives.
Importantly, Russian attacks mostly targeted liquefied gas units, which are usually the most flammable and prone to exploding part of the station, rather than the station’s forecourt. This increases the impact of even a minor hit. For now, it does not seem that these attacks are leading to a systemic fuel shortage, and undamaged stations continue operating without queues or civilians rushing to buy fuel in panic. The consequences of these attacks are largely local and include some temporary closures of petrol stations, changes to their operating schedules, and potentially heavier traffic at the undamaged stations. To this point, the apparent goal seems to be to make civilians afraid of routine tasks such as refueling their cars, rather than to constrain fuel supply in these areas.
These strikes fit into the broader pattern of Russia’s targeting of critical civilian infrastructure in Ukraine. Other examples are attacks on water supply systems such as the Pecheniky dam in the Kharkiv region. These strikes only further exacerbate the already dire humanitarian situation of Ukraine’s front line communities, where relentless attacks have wiped out entire cities, displaced much of the population, and left those who remain — often the elderly and people with limited mobility — with limited access to basic services. Whether through spreading fear among the civilian population or actually decreasing their access to fuel, these strikes — alongside the direct civilian targeting along the front line — appear designed to make these areas even less livable.
