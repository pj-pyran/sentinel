Crisis / situation: Yemen

--- Report 1: IOM (2026-11-18) ---
SITUATION OVERVIEW
Yemen’s security and humanitarian situation worsened significantly throughout September, driven by the continued spread of ground fighting following August’s violent escalations. Ground hostilities expanded rapidly across southwestern fronts, with Ansar Allah (AA) forces intensifying offensives in Ta’iz and Lahj as they attempted to consolidate gains on the West Coast by securing the overlooking mountains. The fighting triggered a massive surge in displacement, forcing over 160,140 individuals to flee their homes, particularly from the Maqbanah, Jabal Habashi, and Al Ma’afer districts in Ta’iz. Vital supply lines, including the crucial road linking Mokha to Ta’iz City, were severely disrupted and cut off, stranding communities and impacting humanitarian access. The AA forces claimed to have targeted several sites in Riyadh and the Aramco facilities in Yanbu, a Saudi Red Sea port, with missiles and drones. Saudi Arabia requested air defenses for critical facilities from Egypt, France, Pakistan, and the UK. French President Emmanuel Macron said France would send military assets to help protect Yanbu following the attack, as the concerns over maritime security and global oil prices grew. Saudi Arabia also reportedly urged the Government of Yemen (GoY) to call for a general mobilization, which was announced in a speech marking the anniversary of the September 26 Revolution. On the other side, in a move to capitalize on its capture of the Bab al-Mandeb and the West Coast, the AA-affiliated Red Sea Ports and Tax Customs Authorities announced reductions in vessel and container fees and port taxes, effective 1 October. The sharp escalation in violence severely strained Yemen’s already compromised health infrastructure, with hospitals in Ta’iz, Ma’rib, and Hadramaut overwhelmed by casualties. Major health centers were forced to suspend non-emergency care—such as maternity and obstetrics services—to reallocate scarce beds and medical personnel toward trauma management. The cholera epidemic maintained a strong hold across southern and western governorates, with transmission exacerbated by population displacement and damaged water and sanitation infrastructure. Abyan and Lahj continued to report the highest concentration of suspected cases, while newly displaced clusters in Ta’iz and Aden saw rising incidence rates. According to the IPC, nearly five million people faced crisis levels of hunger or worse in government-controlled areas between March and May, including 1.4 million in emergency conditions, and it has been observed that food security has significantly deteriorated since the escalation began. Wheat flour prices increased by 10 per cent in parts of Ta’iz as of late September, while traders estimate wheat stocks will cover only about two months of demand. At the UN General Assembly, 80 countries supported a statement condemning “the Houthis’ decision to resume the conflict in Yemen, their continuous strikes on Saudi Arabia, and their reckless attacks on the Red Sea.” UN Special Envoy Hans Grundberg met with AA negotiators in Muscat to avoid further escalation and held talks in Cairo with the regional powers, including Egypt and the League of Arab States.

--- Report 2: IOM (2026-11-18) ---
SITUATION OVERVIEW
Yemen’s security and humanitarian crisis worsened in October with the expanding ground offensives, intensifying cross-border air strikes, and severe strains on already fragile structures for essential services such as healthcare. Ansar Allah (AA) forces sustained their multi-axis offensives across southwestern fronts, continuing heavy assaults on the key frontlines in Taiz while launching multiple strikes at strategic structures including the Presidential Palace and International Airport in Aden. This led to large-scale movements, driving the cumulative number of newly displaced individuals to over 300,000 since June. Taiz became the main frontline and humanitarian concern when AA forces captured Al-Turbah and AsSafiyah, cutting the main Taiz–Aden Road and effectively encircling Taiz City. Key commercial and humanitarian transport corridors remained blocked or compromised, with severe access restrictions critically isolating the population in Taiz. AA forces expanded long-range ballistic missile and drone strikes against the International Airports, oil infrastructures, and military installations across Saudi Arabia, and Saudi-led coalition forces alongside the Government of Yemen (GoY) also intensified air strikes against key AA positions in Yemen. As of 27 October, OHCHR had documented 263 civilian casualties, including 67 people killed. Children accounted for 97 of the casualties and about half of those killed. With the surge in conflict casualties, major referral hospitals across Taiz, Lahj, Marib, and Hadramawt faced severe shortages of basic medical supplies, fuel, and medical oxygen, and were forced to suspend routine and elective care to prioritize trauma operations. The health emergency was compounded by damaged water treatment systems and overcrowded, unsanitary conditions in displacement sites across the top concentrated governorates such as Lahj, Abyan, Taiz, and Aden. Food security exacerbated as staple commodities and fuel prices increased steeply with the continuing supply chain blockades and worsening access situation. Depleted humanitarian stockpiles and severe underfunding raise concerns of acute malnutrition cases increasing, as estimated 5.4 million people in GoY-controlled areas are expected to be in IPC Phase 3 or worse food insecurity in the final quarter of the year. Both GoY and AA raised fuel prices in their respective areas of control in the last week of October, with official price of gasoline ranging between YR 570 per liter (AA-controlled area, 20% increase since midSeptember), YR 1,700 per liter (GoY-controlled area), and over YR 2,500 per liter in Taiz. The citizens are forced to purchase fuel in the black market, where petrol prices skyrocketed to over YR 150,000 for a 20-liter can, as most gas stations have shut down. Water production in Taiz city has fallen by about half due to the critical shortage of fuel.

--- Report 3: FEWS NET (2026-11-17) ---
FEWS NET assesses a credible risk of Famine (IPC Phase 5) in Ta’izz City starting during February-May 2027 in the event of prolonged and tightened siege conditions. Since July, conflict in Yemen has escalated into a high-intensity, multi-front war marked by fluid battle lines, localized territorial shifts, and deepening regional involvement, with Ta’izz and Lahij governorates emerging as the epicenter of fighting (Figure 1). On October 4, the Sana’a-based authorities (SBA) captured the last major road linking Ta’izz City to government-held areas, effectively enclosing the city under a de facto siege. An estimated 600,000-750,000 people, many already displaced to the city from other locations, face worsening shortages of food, fuel, cooking gas, and water. Under the most likely scenario, limited quantities of food and other essentials will likely continue to be smuggled into the city via small mountain roads, sustaining Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) outcomes. However, if fighting for control of the city persists for several months or longer and if siege conditions tighten – further restricting already limited food supply inflows and generating price spikes that render food increasingly unaffordable – then extreme food deprivation would likely drive acute malnutrition and hunger-related mortality above the Famine (IPC Phase 5) thresholds.
Prior to the siege, residents of Ta’izz City had already depleted much of their coping capacity after more than a decade of sustained conflict and economic contraction. Competition for limited informal labor opportunities and macroeconomic instability have placed substantial pressure on household budgets and resulted in widespread slight-to-moderate food consumption gaps over multiple years. Post-harvest food security data collected in late 2025 and analyzed in early 2026 indicated considerable food consumption deficits across the city’s three districts, even during a time of the year when seasonal improvements in food and income are typically observed as labor opportunities tied to agricultural value chains increase and prices decline modestly. At least 20 percent of surveyed households had a poor Food Consumption Score (FCS), while Household Hunger Scale (HHS) results indicated moderate to severe hunger among at least 20 percent of surveyed households, indicative of Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse. Even larger shares of the population reported using food consumption and livelihood coping strategies associated with Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse outcomes, such as reducing meal size and frequency, reducing health expenditures, sending children to work, and sending family members to eat elsewhere.
Household food deprivation likely became more widespread and severe as intensifying conflict coincided with the peak of the lean season in September, before the siege began in October. Although recent representative Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) survey data are unavailable for Ta’izz City, estimates modeled by the Yemen Nutrition Cluster using historical nutrition treatment admissions data suggest that GAM prevalence among children under five would likely remain within Critical levels (15-29.9 percent) prior to the siege. High levels of acute malnutrition are driven by a combination of inadequate food intake, poor water, sanitation, and hygiene conditions, suboptimal infant and young child feeding practices, and inadequate access to health and nutrition services, in addition to reduced household spending on healthcare to prioritize food purchases.
Under the most likely scenario, the siege of Ta’izz City is expected to persist in the near term but is not expected to be protracted. Limited quantities of food and other essentials are expected to continue entering the city through informal mountain routes, preventing a complete collapse in food availability while leaving markets severely disrupted. Prices will likely remain well above average and increasingly unaffordable due to constrained supply, the high costs of smuggling goods into the city, and falling household incomes. Given severely limited household purchasing power and coping capacity, Emergency (IPC Phase 4) outcomes are expected to persist, with a rising portion of the worst-affected households in Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5). While available information suggests that current siege conditions are less restrictive than during the peak of the 2015 siege on Ta’izz City, households now have substantially fewer resources to withstand them. Interviews with multiple key informants indicate that severe food consumption deficits are already emerging among poor households, who are reducing meal size and frequency, prioritizing feeding children at the expense of adults, and selling off their few remaining assets to purchase food and other basic needs. Some poor households are reportedly scavenging from accumulating trash, while even middle- and better-off households are increasingly relying on dwindling, overstretched support from relatives and community networks. Worsening sanitation conditions due to the lapse of waste collection services, coupled with shortages of medicines and medical supplies, are expected to increase communicable disease risks and constrain treatment capacity at a time of year when disease incidence is already typically elevated following the rainy season. The interaction between worsening food consumption deficits and disease is likely to accelerate deterioration in nutritional status, particularly among children, pregnant and lactating women, and the elderly.
Under the credible alternative scenario, the siege would persist for several months or longer while restrictions on remaining supply routes progressively tighten, substantially reducing or effectively cutting off food inflows. Food shortages would become increasingly severe as traders become unwilling or unable to transport goods through active conflict zones and checkpoints. As existing stocks become depleted, remaining market supplies would become prohibitively expensive for poor households, while collapsing income-earning opportunities and the exhaustion of coping options would make it increasingly likely that at least 20 percent of the population would experience extreme food consumption deficits consistent with Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5). With modeled estimates already indicating Critical levels of acute malnutrition prior to the siege, sustained severe food deprivation, compounded by worsening disease exposure and increasingly limited access to healthcare and nutrition treatment, would likely drive GAM prevalence beyond the Famine (IPC Phase 5) threshold. The combined effects of severe malnutrition, disease, and diminished treatment capacity would be expected to drive hunger- and disease-related mortality beyond the Famine (IPC Phase 5) threshold.
