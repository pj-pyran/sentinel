Crisis / situation: Yemen

--- Report 1: FAO (2026-08-19) ---
SHORT-TERM OUTLOOK AND IMPLICATIONS ON FOOD SECURITY:
Yemen remains in the grip of a severe humanitarian crisis, with over 53% of its population projected in IPC Phase 3+ during the June–September 2026 lean season and millions enduring IPC Phase 4 Emergency conditions—the highest global burden. Exacerbated by extreme El Niño climate impacts—where rainfall in key southern, central, and western agricultural zones is expected to drop more than 80% below normal alongside temperatures up to 3°C above average—the crisis directly threatens rainfed crops, pasture, and water resources. Compounding this, food basket and fuel prices have remained stable but high level in a nation heavily reliant on imports for over 80% of its staple foods, leaving households vulnerable to currency depreciation and Red Sea trade bottlenecks. These escalating needs coincide with a critical funding deficit, as the 2026 humanitarian appeal remained about 20% funded by August, forcing WFP and partners to scale back emergency life-saving operations. This stark regional shortfall reflects broader global patterns seen in the SOFI 2026 report, where despite a gradual decline in global hunger to 7.8% in 2025, persistent regional conflicts and energy price shocks continue to disproportionately inflate the cost of nutrient-dense foods across Africa and the Middle East.
KEY HIGHLIGHTS:

EXCHANGE RATES: The exchange rate in GoY-controlled areas held steady at about YER 1,555 per USD in July. This level is 82% stronger than in July 2025 and 31% higher than the three year average, which helped ease short term exchange rate pressure on import prices.
FOOD PRICES: Staple food prices were broadly stable in July: sunflower oil rose 2%, kidney beans fell 1%, and rice and wheat flour were essentially unchanged. All four commodities remained 24–34% below July 2025 levels and 1–16% below their three year averages.
MINIMUM FOOD BASKET (MFB): The Minimum Food Basket held steady at YER 132,678 in July—32% lower than in July 2025 and 12% below the three year average—yet irregular incomes and weak employment continued to constrain household access to food.
FOOD AND FUEL IMPORTS: Recorded wheat and wheat flour imports declined 19% from June but were still 95% higher than in July 2025 and 59% above the three year average. Fuel imports increased 15% month on month yet remained 68% below last year and 65% below the three year average; recorded flows may not reflect national stocks or actual availability.
FUEL PRICES: Diesel prices fell 4% in July and were flat year on year, yet remained 32% above the three year average. Petrol rose 2% month on month, stood 7% below last year, and was 13% higher than the three year average.
TERMS OF TRADE (ToT): Agricultural labor-to-cereal terms of trade (ToT) eased slightly in July, down 3% month on month, but remained 36% above last year and 30% above the three year average. Casual labor-to-cereal ToT fell by about 1% month on month, yet stayed 18% higher than last year and 6% above the three year average.
CASUAL LABOR WAGE RATES: Agricultural wages fell 3% in July, leaving them 5% below July 2025 but 8% above the three year average. Unskilled wages were largely unchanged, yet remained 22% below last year and 9% below the three year average, underscoring ongoing pressure on daily workers’ earnings.
LIVESTOCK PRICES: Goat and sheep prices fell a further 3% and 4% respectively after June’s declines. Both are roughly 31–32% below July 2025 levels and about 4% under their three year averages, eroding the cash value of livestock assets.

--- Report 2: IOM (2026-08-18) ---
SITUATION OVERVIEW
Frontlines in Yemen witnessed an uptick in reinforcements as government forces raised combat readiness and received ammunition and arms support from Saudi Arabia amidst heightened tensions with Ansar Allah (AA). During this period, AA announced a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia, threatening to interdict vessels bound for Saudi ports and potentially close the Bab al Mandeb under their ‘eye for an eye’ policy. This escalation followed airstrikes on Sana’a Airport, designed to prevent AA from establishing a direct air route between Tehran and Sana’a. AA missile and drone strikes targeting Saudi oil tankers and Saudilinked vessels continued throughout the final weeks of July, while also hitting Saudi Aramco oil facilities along the Red Sea coast. The deterioration of maritime security led to an increase in global oil prices and reduced commercial traffic through the Bab alMandeb strait. The UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2826, extending reporting requirements on AA maritime attacks in the Red Sea through January 2027. Concurrently, UN Special Envoy Hans Grundberg maintained continuous diplomatic travel between Muscat and Riyadh, briefing regional leadership and UN Security Council members on preserving the political framework of the 2022 UN-brokered truce.
On July 1, Saudi Arabia delivered a first batch of emergency generators to Hadramawt under the Saudi Development and Reconstruction Program for Yemen (SDRPY). However, electricity shortages persisted, with power outages in Aden reaching six hours of blackout for every two hours of electricity. The General Union of Southern Workers’ Syndicates organized a protest outside UN headquarters in Khormaksar on July 20 to demand improved public services. Meanwhile, local media reported that food prices rose in Sana’a and other AA-controlled areas as a result of an estimated 400 per cent rise in customs duties on flour and an additional levy of YR 5,000 imposed on each carton or sack of imported goods. Torrential downpours further exacerbated humanitarian conditions, especially for populations in Sana’a, Ibb, Amran, Al Hodeidah, Taiz and other areas. Heavy rainfall and windstorms also caused widespread damage to shelters across IOM-managed IDP sites, increasing humanitarian needs beyond available response capacity.
On July 20, the National Defense Council approved military, security, economic, and administrative measures to strengthen state institutions and boost state revenue, including a decision to resume oil exports. Oil production had been halted since October 2022, and years of conflict have damaged critical infrastructure including wells, pipelines and refineries, posing significant challenges to a speedy recovery. Regardless, anticipated proceeds from oil sales are to be deposited into the government-run Central Bank of Yemen in Aden to stabilize the currency and support essential government spending.
