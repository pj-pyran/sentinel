Crisis / situation: Bangladesh

--- Report 1: UNHCR (2026-08-11) ---
This snapshot provides an overview of how UNHCR listens and responds to Rohingya refugees’ questions, concerns, and feedback in Bangladesh between July and December 2025. It focuses on the Complaints and Feedback Mechanism (CFM), which allows refugees to share issues, seek information, and report problems through multiple safe and accessible channels, including helpdesks and a toll-free helpline. In Bangladesh, the CFM operates through the Centralized Automated, Information Processing Platform (CAIPP), which helps ensure automated referral of the issues, the provision of information provided by the service provider, supported by a FAQs messages library for reliable information, and access to needed services supporting UNHCR’s commitment to transparency, accountability to affected populations (AAP) and responsive humanitarian action.

--- Report 3: UNHCR (2026-08-09) ---
1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
PROTECTION ENVIRONMENT SNAPSHOT
As of March 2026, some 1.19 million Rohingya refugees lived in highly congested camps in Cox’s Bazar and on the island of Bhasan Char. The population remained extremely vulnerable: women and children comprised 78%, and more than 136,800 people had specific needs, including nearly 100,000 persons with disabilities. Restrictions on movement, livelihoods, skills development, and education kept refugees highly dependent on humanitarian assistance.
Rohingya continued fleeing violence in Rakhine State. Following government approval of biometric identification of the new arrivals, 146,989 newly arrived Rohingya were identified between January 2025 and March 2026. The Government maintained a closed border policy, and UNHCR recorded 778 refoulement incidents in Q1. Despite ongoing border monitoring and engagement with authorities, interventions succeeded in only 9% of cases.
Serious security incidents rose by 14% from the previous quarter, including abductions, kidnapping for ransom, and forced recruitment. Gender-based violence, especially intimate partner violence, and child protection incidents remained high. Armed groups and organized criminal gangs continued to undermine security and the civilian and humanitarian character of the camps.
Access to justice remained limited by weak accountability mechanisms, adherence to rule-of-law, lengthy procedures, documentation gaps, and delays in biometric identification. Fear of retaliation also deterred reporting, with 64% of gender-based violence survivors declining legal services.
Human trafficking incidents increased sharply, reflecting worsening protection risks and greater exposure to exploitation. Land and ownership disputes, perceived service disparities, and limited shared spaces continued to strain social cohesion with host communities.
Funding cuts reduced staffing capacity across UNHCR and partners by about 30%, increasing pressure on remaining teams to manage expanded responsibilities while maintaining time and quality standards. Despite this, the operation maintained controls to safeguard integrity and uphold case-processing standards.
