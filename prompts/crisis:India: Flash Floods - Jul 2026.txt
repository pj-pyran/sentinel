Crisis / situation: India: Flash Floods - Jul 2026

--- Report 1: IFRC (2026-08-06) ---
What happened, where and when?
A. Overview of the Assam Flood 2026
The Assam floods began on 19 July 2026, triggered by exceptionally heavy monsoonal rainfall, which reached up to 490% above normal levels in parts of Upper Assam. Rising river levels and continuous rainfall caused floodwaters to spread rapidly across multiple districts, inundating villages, agricultural land, and critical infrastructure.
The situation worsened throughout the following week as flooding expanded, and the number of affected people increased steadily. The emergency reached its peak on 27-28 July, when more than 800 villages were inundated and flood impacts were at their highest.
Charaideo, Sivasagar, and Jorhat emerged as some of the worst-affected districts, experiencing widespread village inundation and damage to livelihoods and agriculture.
Since late July, receding river levels and improving weather conditions have led to a gradual improvement in the flood situation.
However, despite the overall decline in flood impacts, 1,144,733 people remain affected across eight districts, with floodwaters still impacting 437 villages. While the crisis has moved beyond its peak, significant humanitarian and recovery needs persist in the most affected areas.
The floods have severely impacted communities that rely primarily on agriculture, livestock and daily wage labour for their livelihoods.
Prolonged inundation has restricted access to essential services, damaged household assets, disrupted agricultural activities and increased public health concerns related to water-borne and vector-borne diseases. The disaster has also resulted in significant loss of livestock and agricultural production, further reducing the coping capacity of already vulnerable households.

--- Report 2: HAI (2026-08-05) ---
CURRENT SITUATION

A week after the floods, thousands of families remain unable to return to normal life. Homes are buried under silt and debris, livelihoods have been destroyed, access to safe water and sanitation remains limited, and many households continue to live in temporary shelters with little means to recover.
The disaster has created a prolonged recovery crisis beyond immediate relief. Many affected communities, particularly those NOT traditionally flood-prone, lost homes, agricultural land, livestock, and essential household assets built over years, with local communities estimating that full recovery may take 5-10 years without sustained support.
The response is now shifting to early recovery and rehabilitation. Immediate priorities include shelter repair, debris and silt clearance, restoration of livelihoods, WASH and public health support, psychosocial care, and rebuilding community resilience, requiring coordinated support from government, humanitarian organisations, donors, and the private sector.

--- Report 3: HAI (2026-08-05) ---
ASSAM (19 JULY - PRESENT)

A total of 87 deaths have been confirmed, and more than 705,000 people have been affected, with Sivasagar being the worst-hit district. Thousands of people remain displaced, many still living in roadside shelters or unable to return home after losing their houses, livestock, and belongings.
A total of 56,606 hectares of agricultural land has been affected, with large areas covered by sand and silt, making cultivation difficult for the coming seasons. Many homes remain filled with debris that families are unable to clear on their own, while limited access to safe drinking water is increasing the risk of disease.
Many people remain missing, with concerns some may still be buried under debris and silt. This has increased the need for drone assessments to support search efforts.
Response is now shifting from immediate relief to early recovery, focusing on shelter repair, essential household items, debris clearance, livelihood support, WASH, health and psychosocial care. The scale of the crisis continues to stretch existing response capacity.
