Crisis / situation: Syrian Arab Republic

--- Report 1: Health Cluster (2026-10-04) ---
SITUATION OVERVIEW
Core monthly subjects:
Service continuity became the central operational issue: the transition of health services from fragmented humanitarian and NGO arrangements toward national management, particularly in the northeast and in areas where NGO support was ending. Fuel costs, transport, staffing, medicine supply, equipment maintenance and referral access had become the clearest sources of operational pressure. Northeast transition risk centered on staffing, authority, referral pathways, ambulance movement, supplies and road access rather than a complete breakdown of services. Fuel pressure affected generators, ambulances, staff transport, water pumping and trucking, cold chain, medical logistics and household access to care. Essential medicines, dialysis consumables, diagnostics, oxygen, oncology and equipment maintenance remained high-priority continuity risks.
Trauma, EMS and referral readiness remained critical: Explosive ordnance and ammunition incidents, serious road crashes, localized violence and referral constraints repeatedly kept trauma readiness at or near the top of the priority lists. There is a need for facility-level surge readiness rather than an assumption that national capacity can absorb localized shocks. Maintain ambulance, blood, surgical, burn, ICU and alternate referral capacity in exposed areas. Referral networks are particularly vulnerable where insecurity, road restrictions, fuel costs and limited receiving capacity overlap. Explosive-risk management remains a health-sector issue because casualty events can rapidly overwhelm individual facilities.
Public-health surveillance stayed in a high-vigilance posture: Vaccination, measles and polio surveillance were critical and shifted toward campaign verification, missed-child identification and catch-up activity. Later updates continued to prioritize measles case finding and vaccination, while waterborne disease, acute watery diarrhoea, foodborne illness and hepatitis A remained medium-high vulnerabilities requiring investigation if local clusters appeared. Key VPD triggers: rash-fever clusters, missed or zero-dose child pockets, cold-chain interruption, AFP alerts and school or returnee-community clustering. Key WASH triggers: linked severe diarrhoeal cases, unsafe shared water, interrupted pumping or chlorination, or a localized jaundice/hepatitis cluster. Malnutrition remained a chronic high-pressure issue, especially where food costs, diarrhoeal disease and nutrition-supply interruptions overlap.
