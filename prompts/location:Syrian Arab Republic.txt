Crisis / situation: Syrian Arab Republic

--- Report 1: Protection Cluster (2026-08-24) ---
The Syrian conflict has resulted in one of the largest displacement crises in recent history, with currently an estimated 5.5 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) across the country. Over the years, shifting frontlines, ceasefires, and changes in territorial control have influenced patterns of displacement and prospects for return. In late 2024, the collapse of the regime in Syria (December 8, 2024) removed a number of major barriers to return. Since then, over 3.7 million people, including IDP returnees and refugee returnees, have returned to their areas of origin. However, these return movements have been fraught with significant protection risks and challenges, primarily due to the limited availability of services and the fragile security and economic context in many areas. Returnees in Syria face a complex landscape of protection risks. Humanitarian actors must maintain a strong field of presence and vigilance to respond to these risks and prevent further harm. One of the most immediate threats is the danger posed by explosive ordnance contamination, which continues to cause injuries and fatalities in areas of return. Additionally, many returnees face homelessness or inadequate shelter, which not only undermines their dignity but also increases their vulnerability to further protection violations. House, land &amp; property (HLP)-related violations are also common, especially when returnees attempt to reclaim homes that have been occupied by others during their displacement. These disputes often lead to forced evictions, threats, intimidation, or other forms of retaliation, further undermining prospects for safe, voluntary, and dignified return. Safety and security concerns are widespread, with returnees at risk of arbitrary detention, extortion, abduction, and exposure to hostilities. Returnees may also encounter social tensions and discrimination, particularly in communities where resources are scarce or where political and sectarian divisions persist. These tensions can hinder reintegration and fuel further marginalization. Access to essential services, such as food, water, sanitation, healthcare, and education, is often limited. This is frequently due to the lack of civil documentation, which restricts individuals' ability to access public services and legal remedies in a context where the legal system is weak or non-functional. Compounding these challenges is the issue of missing or expired personal and family documents, including the registration of births and deaths, which further limits access to rights and services. Furthermore, climate change, economic instability, and other humanitarian needs barriers have significantly worsened the protection environment. Economic hardship due to limited livelihood opportunities, energy shortages, and inflation may lead to harmful coping mechanisms such as child labor, early marriage, school dropouts, and substance abuse and GBV. Vulnerable families are often forced to deprioritize essential needs, including healthcare and nutrition, to afford basic needs. As displacement and return dynamics evolve and increasing numbers of IDPs consider or commence return, there is a growing need for harmonized, principled, and well-coordinated protection interventions.
This Guidance Note seeks to promote a coordinated response across all protection actors, avoid duplication of efforts, and reinforce the centrality of protection in return and broader recovery initiatives. The guidance draws on sub-national practices developed in specific regions of Syria, as well as international standards and best practices from similar contexts, to ensure consistency and contextual relevance at the whole-of-Syria level. It provides unified, national-level operational guidance for the engagement of protection partners on the IDPs’ return across Syria. It covers different return dynamics in all areas of Syria, and support protection partners operating in both areas of displacement and areas of return to uphold the rights, safety, and dignity of IDPs. This Note also seeks to promote a coordinated response across actors, avoid duplication, and promote the centrality of protection in broader return and recovery efforts. Importantly, this guidance is anchored in global protection frameworks – including the UN Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement (which affirm that IDPs’ returns must be voluntary, safe and dignified), the IASC Framework on Durable Solutions for IDPs (endorsed by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee, providing key principles and criteria for sustainable solutions), relevant Global Protection Cluster operational guidance on returns, and UNHCR protection standards. All protection actors should ensure that all returns are voluntary, safe, dignified, and informed, and that protection risks are identified, prevented, mitigated, and responded to at every stage of the return process.
