Crisis / situation: Afghanistan

--- Report 1: IFRC (2026-06-17) ---
A. SITUATION ANALYSIS
Description of the crisis
Since the beginning of 2025 until 30 April 2026, Afghanistan has witnessed the return of more than 3.42 million Afghan nationals (returnees and deportees) from neighbouring countries - one of the largest cross-border population movements recorded in the region since 2021.
According to data published by the International Organization for Migration (IOM) and United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), approximately 2.1 million individuals returned from Iran, while over 1.25 million returned from Pakistan, with smaller but growing movements observed from Tajikistan, Türkiye, and other countries. These movements form part of a longer trend: an estimated 5.8 million Afghans have returned since September 2023, equivalent to a 10–12 per cent increase in the country's population over two years.
The scale, pace, and sustained nature of these returns continue to exacerbate Afghanistan's already severe humanitarian crisis. More than half of returnees arrived through western and eastern border provinces where local infrastructure, health systems, shelter capacity, and livelihood opportunities remain critically overstretched. Humanitarian partners report that despite the winter season traditionally limiting mobility, cross-border returns remained consistently high through late 2025 and into the first quarter of 2026, indicating that deportation policies, documentation pressures, and protection concerns continue to outweigh seasonal constraints on movement.
Returnees from Pakistan
Based on data from UNHCR and Afghan Red Crescent Society (ARCS) border monitoring reports, between 1 January 2025 and 30 April 2026, an estimated 1,335,800 Afghans returned from Pakistan, of whom approximately 208,200 (15 per cent) were forcibly returned or deported.
The return trend accelerated following the implementation of the second phase of the government of Pakistan’s ‘Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan’ (IFRP) in April 2025, initially targeting undocumented Afghans and holders of Afghan Citizen Cards (ACC). On 31 July 2025, the Government of Pakistan announced the expansion of the repatriation plan to include holders of Proof of Registration (PoR) cards, significantly increasing uncertainty among the Afghan population residing in the country.
Conditions for Afghans in Pakistan deteriorated sharply over the reporting period, with increased arrests, detention, evictions, and restrictions on movement and access to services. These pressures generated a high proportion of forced and pre-emptive returns, often undertaken under distressing conditions and with minimal preparation. At the same time, escalating tensions between Pakistan and Afghanistan resulted in cross-border hostilities, intermittent border closures, and disruptions to trade and humanitarian access, producing congestion and unpredictable population movements at key crossings.
According to operational monitoring by the ARCS, the highest daily influx from Pakistan during the reporting period was recorded in November 2025, with more than 9,800 individuals crossing in a single day. Most returnees entered Afghanistan through the official border crossings at Torkham (Nangarhar)and Spin Boldak (Kandahar), while smaller cross-border movements were observed through Angur Ada Border Crossing and informal crossing routes in Helmand province.
Despite temporary fluctuations linked to border closures and political negotiations, daily arrivals remained elevated through the first four months of 2026, suggesting that return movements from Pakistan are likely to continue throughout the remainder of the year.
Returnees from Iran
Returns from Iran continued to represent the largest share of cross-border movements during the reporting period. According to UNHCR and IOM border monitoring reports, between 1 January 2025 and 30 April 2026, an estimated 2,099,600 Afghan nationals returned from Iran, with approximately 1,435,400 deported. Accounting for nearly 62 per cent of all recorded returns during the period.
Of these returns, more than 1.3 million were undocumented or deported individuals, reflecting intensified migration enforcement and reduced access to temporary residency mechanisms inside Iran. Return movements rose sharply between June and September 2025, with daily arrivals at times exceeding 35,000 individuals per day at the Islam Qala Border Crossing in Herat, making it one of the largest single border movements recorded in the region in recent years.
Although daily arrival rates decreased after the mid-year peak, returns remained consistently high through late 2025 and into early 2026. Most returnees entered through Islam Qala and Milak-Zaranj Border Crossing, often arriving with limited belongings, depleted savings, and urgent humanitarian needs.
Humanitarian assessments conducted by UNHCR indicate that many returnee households from Iran face acute vulnerabilities upon arrival, including lack of shelter, loss of livelihoods, absence of civil documentation, and limited access to health care. Women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities, and unaccompanied and separated children continue to face heightened protection risks.
Returnees from Tajikistan
In Tajikistan, entry for Afghan nationals remains limited to those possessing valid passports and visas. At the same time, humanitarian actors, including UNHCR, continue to report cases of arbitrary deportation involving recognized refugees and asylum seekers, heightening concerns about incidences of refoulement.
Between January 2025 and April 2026, approximately 2760 Afghanswere recorded returning from Tajikistan. Although fewer than from Iran and Pakistan, these returns continue to raise serious protection concerns due to legal uncertainty, family separation, and restricted access to asylum procedures.
Humanitarian Outlook
The sustained return of more than 3.4 million people in sixteen months continues to place extraordinary pressure on Afghanistan’s humanitarian response capacity. Border provinces including Herat, Nangarhar, Kandahar, and Nimroz are experiencing increased strain on reception facilities, emergency health services, shelter assistance, and protection referral systems. With deportation policies in neighbouring countries expected to continue throughout 2026, humanitarian actors anticipate that cross-border returns will remain at significant levels, further increasing immediate humanitarian needs and long-term reintegration challenges across Afghanistan.

--- Report 2: OCHA (2026-06-16) ---
Funding shortfalls threaten Afghanistan's humanitarian response
Humanitarian organizations in Afghanistan are facing a growing challenge in responding to widespread needs with increasingly limited resources. Across the country, millions of people are struggling with the combined effects of food insecurity, economic hardship, climate-related shocks, displacement and protection risks. Yet funding for the humanitarian response remains far below what is required to meet these needs.
As of the end of May, the 2026 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan received just US$269 million against a requirement of $1.71 billion, leaving a funding gap of $1.44 billion. This represents a decrease of $110 million compared with funding received at the same time in 2025, despite persisting humanitarian needs across the country.
Despite these constraints, humanitarian partners reached 4.7 million people with at least one form of assistance between January and March 2026. More than 435,000 people received support from three or more sectors, reflecting efforts to address multiple needs simultaneously.
Food assistance accounted for nearly two-thirds of the total reach, supporting around three million people during the first quarter. This scale underscores the severity of food insecurity across Afghanistan, where many families rely on humanitarian assistance to meet their basic needs. At the same time, millions face overlapping vulnerabilities, requiring support in health care, nutrition, clean water, shelter, protection and education.
Cash assistance has also played a vital role in enabling vulnerable households to cope with the crisis. During the first quarter of 2026, humanitarian partners distributed US$20.3 million in cash and voucher assistance, reaching 2.3 million people across 33 provinces. For many families, cash support provides flexibility and dignity, allowing them to prioritize their most urgent needs, whether food, rent, health expenses, or other essential costs.
However, the funding gap is already affecting operations. Humanitarian organizations are being forced to reduce programmes, scale back services, and make difficult prioritization decisions. As a result, many vulnerable people may receive less assistance than planned or may not be reached at all.
The situation is particularly concerning for women and girls, whose access to education, employment and public life remains severely restricted. These limitations continue to deepen vulnerabilities and increase reliance on humanitarian assistance.
Humanitarian partners remain committed to delivering life-saving support across Afghanistan, but sustaining the response will require urgent, flexible and predictable donor funding. Without additional resources, millions of people risk falling deeper into crisis, while humanitarian organizations face increasingly difficult choices about how to allocate scarce assistance.
At a time when needs remain immense, the gap between humanitarian requirements and available funding continues to widen, threatening to undermine hard-won gains and leaving many Afghans without the support they need to weather another year of uncertainty.
Frontline realities in Afghanistan’s east: A region caught between war, floods and fading resources
Afghanistan’s eastern provinces are once again under strain from overlapping crises, conflict, flooding, displacement and the lingering impact of last year’s earthquake. For humanitarian actors, the pattern is becoming familiar: one emergency barely stabilizes before another begins.
In May, OCHA’s Head of Office, Amy Martin, undertook a two-day visit to Nangarhar and Kunar provinces, meeting families affected by recent shocks, local authorities, and humanitarian partners working to respond to rising needs.

A landscape of repeated shocks

In Kunar Province, communities described a layered history of loss. Last year’s earthquake continues to shape daily life, while recent cross-border hostilities have added further destruction. Homes, roads, clinics and schools have been damaged, with reports of civilian casualties and displacement along the border with Pakistan. The result, according to humanitarian officials, is a population repeatedly drawn into crisis, with little time or resources to recover.
At Asadabad Provincial Hospital, the pressure is visible. The facility remains a key referral centre for Kunar and neighboring Nuristan, treating emergency cases and conflict-related casualties. However, staff are operating under severe constraints, including aging infrastructure, limited space, and shortages of specialized equipment, particularly for newborn and emergency care.
During her visit to conflict-affected areas, Amy met families displaced by airstrikes and mortar shelling earlier this year. Communities described widespread civilian harm, including damaged schools, hospitals and homes.
“People are living in fear,” she said, noting that women in particular spoke of the struggle to care for their families amid uncertainty and trauma. She called for a ceasefire and urged all parties to respect international humanitarian law, stressing that civilians and aid workers must not be targeted.
In Nangarhar, flooding has triggered further displacement. Families reported losing homes, water sources, and livelihoods, with many now living in temporary shelters or on agricultural land. While humanitarian assistance has reached many households, communities raised concerns about gaps in coverage and the lack of durable solutions, particularly for those without land or resources to rebuild.
During her visit to flood-affected communities, Amy highlighted how repeated shocks are steadily eroding resilience. Just months after earthquakes displaced thousands, many of the same families are now affected by flooding, with no durable recovery pathways in place. Afghanistan, she said, remains trapped in a cycle of recurring crisis.

A system under financial strain

Across the eastern region, humanitarian partners describe an operating environment stretched to its limits. At a coordination meeting in Jalalabad, agencies warned that multiple emergencies are unfolding simultaneously: conflict displacement, seasonal flooding, disease outbreaks, and large-scale returns from neighboring countries.
But the deeper constraint is financial. Aid organizations said funding cuts are already forcing reductions in food assistance and services for returnees, with some programmes scaling back entirely.
Amy Martin noted that the Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan for Afghanistan remains critically underfunded, with only a fraction of required resources secured. She also pointed to ongoing efforts to unlock stranded humanitarian supplies and improve operational access both essential for sustaining life-saving assistance.
The central message from the mission was clear: needs are rising faster than the ability to respond. Despite the commitment of humanitarian workers and local communities, funding shortfalls and continued insecurity are limiting what can be delivered. Assistance is becoming thinner, slower, and increasingly focused on immediate survival rather than recovery. Without more predictable funding and a reduction in violence, the prospects for durable recovery in eastern Afghanistan remain uncertain. For many families across Nangarhar and Kunar, the crisis is no longer an event. It is the reality of everyday life.
Abdul Naser’s story: A family supported through AHF assistance in Herat
Abdul Naser lives in Khalawak village, in the remote Rubat Sangi district of Herat Province, where life is quiet, but not easy. Surrounded by dry land and vast distances, the village offers few opportunities, especially for someone in Abdul Naser’s situation. “I am facing economic challenges, and I have a disability; I cannot work,” said Abdul Naser.
Both of Abdul Naser’s legs are prosthetic. His disability has taken away his ability to work, and in a place where physical labour is often the only way to earn a living, this has left him with very limited options. Each day is shaped by dependence, patience and the quiet strength to keep going.
“In the house where we live, there are four families and we only have one small room. I live here with my wife and our baby. Life in this house is very difficult; as you can see, we have nothing,” said Abdul Naser.
The small house they live in carries both hope and pressure. The birth of their child brought happiness, but also deepened their worries. Every new life needs care, food and security. Before help arrived, their situation was unstable. Food was uncertain, and each day depended on what little they could manage. His wife did her best to stretch whatever they had, but there were times when even the most basic necessities were missing. Abdul Naser often found himself sitting in silence, carrying the heavy burden of not being able to provide for his family the way he wished. Then, during the blessed month of Ramadan, something changed.
“We received two sacks of flour, eight kilograms of split peas, eight cans of cooking oil and some salt. We are very thankful for the support,” said Abdul Naser. Through humanitarian partner Faradata Social Change and Women Empowerment Organization (FSCWEO), supported by the Afghanistan Humanitarian Fund (AHF), emergency assistance reached their village. For Abdul Naser, receiving flour and other essential supplies brought a sense of relief that had been missing for a long time. This assistance was more than temporary support; it brought stability. He believed it could sustain his small family for up to two months, perhaps even longer if used carefully.
Now, each day, his wife prepares dough from the flour and bakes fresh bread in their simple tandoor, a traditional clay oven. The warmth of the bread fills their home, bringing comfort beyond easing hunger. Yet even in hardship, Abdul Naser chooses generosity.
During Ramadan, he and his wife set aside a portion of the bread and send it to the mosque for iftar. It is their way of sharing kindness and compassion, and staying connected to their community despite having little themselves. For Abdul Naser, dignity is not lost in struggle. It is found in faith, in giving and in continuing forward despite difficult circumstances. In Khalawak village, far from the world's attention, his life continues, quiet, difficult, yet marked by resilience. In that small home, with his wife and child beside him, he holds on to the hope that tomorrow may be just a little better than today.
“If these organizations did not exist, who would provide food for the poor in our village? I sincerely thank FSCWEO,” Abdul Naser observed while reflecting on what the support has meant for his family.
