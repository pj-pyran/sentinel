Crisis / situation: World

--- Report 1: AGRA (2026-11-17) ---
Summary
AGRA’s monthly Food Security Monitor serves as a vital tool for sharing timely data with key stakeholders, supporting informed, evidence-based decisions across the agricultural sector. Below are key highlights from the October 2026 Food Security Monitor edition:
Global Market Updates

Global food commodity prices eased in October 2026 but remained elevated compared with a year earlier, indicating that underlying price pressures persist despite short-term relief. The FAO Food Price Index fell 1.2% to 133.9 points, driven by declines in cereals, dairy and meat, while vegetable oils rose marginally and sugar surged 6.6%; overall food prices remained 5.9% above October 2025 but 16.4% below the March 2022 peak. Similarly, the International Grains Council Grains and Oilseeds Index declined 4.8% month-on-month, with wheat, maize, soybeans and barley all falling, but remained 8.8% above year-earlier levels. Rice was the notable exception, rising 4.0% during the month and 27.6% year-on-year, highlighting continued upward pressure in the rice market despite the broader easing in global grain and oilseed prices
Global fertilizer markets showed mixed but generally firm trends, with phosphate fertilizers and potash strengthening while urea prices eased. DAP, MAP and potash prices increased by 0.7%, 1.3% and 2.2% month-onmonth, respectively, whereas urea declined 2.0% to US$669/MT. Despite this monthly easing, urea remained 11.9% above its year-earlier level, the strongest annual increase among the fertilizers tracked, while DAP, MAP and potash were also higher year-on-year. Overall, fertilizer prices remain elevated, with phosphate and potash markets showing continued upward pressure and the nitrogen segment, particularly urea, remaining more volatile.
Fertilizer price trends across selected African markets remain highly divergent, with Rwanda experiencing the strongest and most persistent price pressures, including year-on-year increases of 40.7% for NPK and 67.1% for urea, while Uganda has seen a recent rebound despite generally weaker annual prices. In Southern Africa, Malawi and Mozambique continue to face elevated year-on-year fertilizer costs despite recent easing in some products, whereas Zambia has recorded modest monthly increases, but prices remain below six-month levels. West African markets appear comparatively softer, with Ghana recording particularly sharp declines in urea prices and Nigeria experiencing broader short- and medium-term price moderation. Overall, fertilizer affordability remains a significant concern in parts of East and Southern Africa, even as easing prices in several markets provide some relief.

Food Commodity Prices Updates

Maize prices exhibited mixed and divergent movements across the monitored regions between September and October in USD terms. In Eastern Africa, prices increased across all monitored markets, signaling broader upward price pressure across the region. South Sudan recorded the strongest increase (+13.0%), followed by Rwanda (+10.6%), Uganda (+8.3%), Ethiopia (+4.8%), Tanzania (+3.5%), and Kenya (+2.1%). South Sudan remained by far the highest-priced market at USD 1,137/MT, while Tanzania remained the lowest at USD 235/MT. Overall, the broadbased increases point to tightening maize availability and sustained regional demand following the earlier postharvest easing, with particularly pronounced pressures in South Sudan, Rwanda, and Uganda.
In Southern Africa also, there is a general price increase, although the magnitude varied considerably across markets. In USD terms, Mozambique recorded a particularly sharp increase (+47.5%) from USD 316/MT to USD 466/MT, signaling significant short-term market pressure, while Malawi rose more moderately (+4.4%) and Zambia remained broadly stable. Local-currency trends similarly showed strong upward pressure in Mozambique (+47.5%), alongside increases in Zambia (+5.2%) and Malawi (+4.7%). Mozambique’s prices were also 44.6% above six-month and 17.1% above year-earlier levels, signaling sustained market tightening. In contrast, despite recent increases, Zambia remained 28.7% below six-month and 11.9% below year-earlier levels, while Malawi was still 18.2% lower year-on-year, indicating that recent upward movements have not fully reversed earlier price easing.
Prices in West Africa exhibited considerable divergence with strong increases in Burkina Faso and Mali contrasting with declines in Nigeria and Niger. In USD terms, Burkina Faso recorded the largest increase (+17.0%), followed by Mali (+9.6%), while Nigeria (-7.5%), Niger (-4.2%), and Togo (-0.9%) declined and Ghana remained broadly stable (+0.7%). Local-currency trends reinforce the strong upward pressure in Mali, where several markets recorded double-digit monthly increases, while selected Burkina Faso markets also rose sharply. Conversely, Niger generally recorded declines, Nigeria fell by 5.8%, and Togo continued to experience broad-based easing. Yearon-year prices nevertheless remained below previous-year levels across many markets, suggesting that localized October increases have not fully reversed the broader regional price normalization.
Rice prices also showed mixed and divergent trends across regions between September and October in USD terms. In East Africa, movements were generally moderate: Tanzania (+1.2%, to USD 835/MT) and Kenya (+0.7%, to USD 1,380/MT) edged up, with Kenya remaining the highest-priced market, while Uganda (-7.0%, to USD 895/MT, due to currency depreciation), South Sudan (-4.1%) and Rwanda (-1.5%) declined. In Southern Africa, Mozambique recorded the sharpest increase (+9.9%, from USD 710/MT to USD 780/MT) as supplies tightened ahead of the lean season, and Malawi rose modestly (+1.1%, to USD 2,125/MT), remaining the highest-priced market at the official exchange rate, while Zambia fell by 8.5% (to USD 1,681/MT), reflecting currency depreciation. In West Africa, prices rose modestly in Mali (+4.6%), Niger (+3.0%), Burkina Faso (+1.6%) and Togo (+0.5%), reflecting poor production prospects and declining stocks in the Sahelian markets, while Nigeria recorded the sharpest decline (-10.6%, to USD 336/MT) amid good market supplies and Ghana was broadly stable (-0.2%, at USD 961/MT) and remained the highest-priced market.

Food Security Updates

Across East Africa, food insecurity is expected to remain severe through May 2027, driven by rainfall deficits and crop losses, conflict and displacement, high staple prices, declining household stocks, and constrained humanitarian assistance. Ethiopia faces worsening conditions in drought- and conflict-affected areas, with pockets of Emergency (IPC Phase 4), while South Sudan remains the region’s most acute crisis, with widespread Emergency outcomes, pockets of Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) and a continuing risk of Famine in some areas. In Kenya, 3.5–4.0 million people are expected to require assistance, although improving rainfall should support gradual recovery in many pastoral areas, while flooding remains a localized threat. Uganda is also expected to remain strained, particularly in Karamoja and refugee settlements, with assistance needs projected to peak at 3.0–3.49 million people in April–May amid poor harvests and high food prices. Overall, seasonal rainfall and harvests will provide some localized improvements, but these gains are likely to be uneven and temporary as conflict, climate shocks, weak purchasing power and reduced humanitarian support sustain high food assistance needs across the region.
In Southern Africa, food insecurity is expected to worsen through the 2026/27 lean season and into 2027, driven by declining household food stocks, weakening purchasing power, high food and transport costs, and increasingly adverse El Niño-related rainfall conditions. Crisis (IPC Phase 3) outcomes are projected across significant parts of Mozambique, Malawi, and Zimbabwe, while Stressed (IPC Phase 2) conditions are expected to expand across Zambia. In Mozambique, conflict and displacement further compound food insecurity in northern areas, while across Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and central and southern Mozambique, below-average and erratic rainfall is expected to constrain planting, crop development, agricultural labour opportunities, livestock production, and the 2027 harvest. Although current and early-season harvests are providing some temporary relief, these gains are expected to diminish as stocks fall and market dependence increases, with the full effects of El Niño likely to become more pronounced after May 2027 and potentially peak during the late-2027 to early-2028 lean season.
Across West Africa, food insecurity is expected to worsen into the 2027 lean season, particularly across conflictaffected parts of the central Sahel and northern Nigeria, where insecurity, displacement, poor or disrupted harvests, rainfall deficits, declining household stocks, weak livelihoods, and high food prices are increasing market dependence. The most severe conditions are projected in Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, and northeastern Nigeria, with Crisis (IPC Phase 3) and Emergency (IPC Phase 4) widespread in affected areas and pockets of Catastrophe (IPC Phase 5) in Mali. In contrast, conditions are generally less severe in Côte d’Ivoire and Togo, where food insecurity remains more localized, although cross-border insecurity, refugee pressures, flooding, and weaker agricultural incomes are increasing vulnerability. Overall, seasonal harvests are providing temporary relief in several countries, but their benefits are expected to fade as stocks decline, with humanitarian food assistance needs rising and generally peaking between March and May 2027.

Update on El Niño conditions

By October 2026, El Niño had strengthened into a very strong event, but its food-security impacts varied considerably across Africa. The Greater Horn faced heightened flooding risks from above-average October– December rainfall, threatening crops, infrastructure, markets and humanitarian access, even as improved rainfall supported pasture and livestock in some areas. Conversely, Southern Africa and parts of unimodal East Africa faced delayed and below-average rainfall, high temperatures and growing risks to crop production, agricultural labour and livestock. West and Central Africa experienced more localized climatic impacts, with conflict, displacement and market disruptions remaining the dominant drivers of acute food insecurity.
The most severe food-security effects were expected to emerge from early 2027 onward, as poor rainfall reduces production, household stocks decline and dependence on increasingly expensive markets grows. Southern and unimodal East Africa face particularly elevated risks from January to June 2027, while flooding could cause more immediate disruptions in the Greater Horn. Across the continent, conflict and displacement are expected to magnify climate shocks, especially in Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, the Sahel, eastern DRC and northern Mozambique. Overall, the outlook points to rising humanitarian needs through 2027, with the severity ultimately determined by rainfall performance, harvest outcomes, staple-price trends, market functioning, humanitarian access and the scale and timeliness of national and international responses.

Food Trade Updates

Ethiopia has officially joined the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS), enabling its financial institutions, businesses, and consumers to participate more efficiently in cross-border payments across Africa and strengthening the country’s integration into the continent’s financial and intra-African trade ecosystem. The National Bank of Ethiopia and PAPSS also announced that Addis Ababa will host PAPSS COWRY 2026 on 26–27 November, bringing together African and global payment leaders to advance continental payment connectivity, innovation, and economic integration.
Ghana became the first country to sign the Accra Convention on Negotiable Cargo Documents, alongside 25 other countries, establishing a harmonised legal framework that allows cargo documents across road, rail, air and sea transport to serve as documents of title. The Convention aims to expand access to trade finance, particularly for SMEs, facilitate cross-border trade, strengthen supply chains and support trade digitalisation, while advancing AfCFTA objectives. Ghana has committed to early ratification, reviewing relevant domestic laws and piloting the new system with transport, customs and financial institutions ahead of implementation.

--- Report 2: World Vision (2026-11-16) ---
SITUATION OVERVIEW
Cambodia continues to experience the impacts of flooding following prolonged heavy rainfall associated with low-pressure systems and seasonal weather patterns. According to the National Committee for Disaster Management (NCDM), flooding has affected 10 provinces, 53 districts and municipalities, and 255 communes and sangkats, impacting approximately 73,922 households nationwide.
As of 6 November 2026, flood conditions across Cambodia have generally improved, with floodwaters steadily receding in most affected areas. Nevertheless, the recent influence of Typhoon No. 31 (In-Fa) has generated moderate to heavy rainfall over the past two days, resulting in localized flash flooding in several districts, particularly along the Cambodia-Thailand border. Water levels in key waterways, including the Sangke River (Battambang Province) and the Mongkul Borey Stream (Banteay Meanchey Province), have increased in response to the rainfall. According to the Tonle Sap Authority, these rivers and streams remain under close observation due to elevated flows and the potential risk to lowlying communities, agricultural lands, and local infrastructure.
Furthermore, the Ministry of Water Resources and Meteorology (MOWRAM) forecasts that the ongoing development of Typhoon No. 31 (In-Fa), combined with monsoonal conditions, may continue to increase precipitation across Cambodia, necessitating continued preparedness and monitoring by relevant authorities and at-risk communities.
