Crisis / situation: World

--- Report 1: IOM (2026-11-24) ---
In late 2015, IOM launched a Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) Flow Monitoring system, aiming to monitor Mixed Migration Flows to Europe by land and sea. Since then, DTM gathers, validates and disseminates information on migrants and refugees entering Europe through four main routes:

Eastern Mediterranean route (EMR): arrivals by sea and land to Bulgaria, Cyprus and Greece
Central Mediterranean route (CMR): arrivals by sea to Italy and Malta
Western Mediterranean route (WMR): arrivals by sea to the Peninsular Coasts of Spain, Balearic Islands, by land to the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla
Western African Atlantic route (WAAR): arrivals by sea to the Canary Islands of Spain

DTM also monitors data on migrants in transit through the Western Balkan route (WBR), including data from Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, and Kosovo (UNSCR 1244, 1999). These are considered separately to prevent, to the extent possible, the double counting of the same people, crossing multiple borders. Finally, DTM compiles data on migrants entering Croatia, Romania and Slovenia by land, typically after having crossed the Western Balkans or Bulgaria.
This report presents the latest available data and trends, focusing on the third quarter of 2026 and comparing it with the same period in 2025.

In the third quarter of 2026 (Q3 2026), 36,097 migrants arrived in Europe via these four routes. This represents a 42 per cent increase from previous Q2 2026 and a 24 per cent decline compared with Q3 2025. The steepest year-on-year drop was on the CMR (down 59%), followed by the EMR (down 13%). Arrivals were distributed across the CMR to Italy and Malta (24%), the EMR to Greece, Cyprus and Bulgaria (42%), the WMR to Spain (25%), and the WAAR to Spain (9%).
According to Eurostat, EU+ countries registered over 99,385 new asylum applications in the third quarter of 2026. Although data on asylum applications remain incomplete at the time of writing, France, Italy and Greece are among the highest ranking countries in the EU27+ in Q3 of 2026. Notably, France registered nearly twice as many first-time asylum applications (27,910) than Italy (15,450) did. Between July and September 2026, the main applicants held nationalities from Afghanistan (10%), Bangladesh (7%) and Sudan (6%), comprising nearly a quarter of all applications registered. These groups are followed by Türkiye, and Pakistan, Ukraine, Syrian Arab Republic and Somalia, averaging at around 4 per cent, respectively.
According to IOM’s MMP, 920 migrants died or went missing at sea while trying to reach Europe through the Mediterranean and Western African Atlantic routes in Q3 of 2026. Of these, 141 perished on the Central Mediterranean, and 350 while trying to cross the Atlantic to reach the Canary Islands. This represents a 6 per cent increase compared to Q3 2025 (871).

--- Report 2: Insecurity Insight (2026-11-23) ---
The Missing Gender Dimension: Sex Is Unknown for Nearly 6 in 10 Health Workers Affected by Violence
Women accounted for 24% and men 76% of health workers affected by violence in cases where sex was reported. But sex was known in only 41% of 15,146 cases recorded by Insecurity Insight (2016-Sept 2026). These figures cannot tell us who faces greater risks. Better sex-disaggregated data is essential to understanding gendered impacts of violence against health workers.
Understanding how armed conflict affects women and men differently requires reliable sex-disaggregated data. Without this information, the gendered impacts of violence against health workers remain largely invisible, limiting our ability to understand who is most affected, assess differences in exposure to violence and develop responses that address people centred needs. The importance of gender-sensitive evidence has long been recognised in the Women, Peace and Security agenda, beginning with UN Security Council Resolution 1325 (2000). Subsequent commitments, including Resolution 1889 (2009) and the Secretary-General’s 2010 report, further emphasised the need for systematic monitoring and the collection of sex- and age-disaggregated data.
Sex is reported for only 41% of health workers affected by violence
Insecurity Insight's monitoring shows that of the 15,146 health workers recorded killed, injured, kidnapped and arrested between January 2016 and September 2026, sex was reported for 6,167 (41%). Reporting rates of sex also varied by incident type, reaching 61% for health workers who were killed, compared with 25% for those who were injured. One reason for these reporting gaps is that many organisations omit sex to protect the privacy and confidentiality of affected health workers and reduce the risk of their identification. While this is an important ethical and protection measure, it limits the availability of sex-disaggregated data and, consequently, the ability to understand gendered patterns of violence against health workers. This presents a challenge for efforts to improve monitoring while ensuring that the safety and privacy of affected individuals remain paramount.
Available data cannot establish whether women and men experience violence differently
Among the 6,167 health workers whose sex was reported, 1,410 (24%) were women and 4,419 (76%) were men. However, these figures should be interpreted with caution. As sex was reported for only 41% of recorded cases where health workers were killed, injured, kidnapped and arrested, the available data cannot establish whether this distribution reflects the actual gender composition of affected health workers or differences in reporting. Drawing conclusions about the relative exposure of women and men to violence based on these figures alone risks producing a misleading picture. It is also important to consider how many women and men work in healthcare overall. Without knowing the gender composition of the health workforce, it is impossible to determine whether women or men face a greater risk of violence. These findings highlight the need for more systematic and ethically responsible approaches to collecting sex-disaggregated data, so policymakers can better understand the gendered impacts of violence against health workers and develop evidence-based responses.
