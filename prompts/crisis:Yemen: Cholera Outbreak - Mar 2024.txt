Crisis / situation: Yemen: Cholera Outbreak - Mar 2024

--- Report 1: Health Cluster (2026-10-30) ---
Highlights
• Daily casualties are falling, but deaths are not. The HEOC register reached 9,072 (7,395 injured, 1,677 deaths) by 25 October, +559 in six days. Daily totals declined from 153 on 20 October to 27 on 24 October before rising again to 56 on 25 October. The period average of ~93 per day is about half that of Sitrep #8 (168/day).
• Marib is the most lethal front. Marib recorded 45 deaths among 143 casualties (31%), more than Taiz (34 of 237) and Lahj (21 of 158). On 22 and 23 October, deaths in Marib outnumbered injuries.
• Report of Health care came under fire again in Taiz. On 20 October HEOC reported Al-Arous Hospital (Sabir Al-Mawadim) was hit by mortar fragments, and an ambulance was destroyed by a projectile, killing its driver and paramedic.
• Shabwah's toll is being revised upwards. The Shabwah Health Office now reports 69 deaths and 166 injured from the 19 October missile strike on Al-Alam Camp near Ataq, against 205 casualties (47 deaths) in the HEOC register. Twenty-one critical patients were transferred to Mukalla.
• Displacement reached 276,301 people (46,044 HH) by 24 October (IOM DTM). New arrivals have slowed sharply since mid-October, but Taiz still hosts 46% of IDPs. Marib's primary care network is significantly weakened: 6 MMTs remain of 31 in 2025.
• Cholera continues to decline from its W40 peak in the five most-affected governorates (639 in W40, 447 in W42), but 3 confirmed cases and 1 death in Marib point to spread beyond the southern belt. WHO has prioritised 29 districts for an ICG request for oral cholera vaccine.
• WHO delivered 56 trauma and emergency kits to Taiz city on 24 October (46 TESK, 10 IEHK) with medicines and MCH supplies, about 30 MT in total, to three hospitals and one health centre.

--- Report 2: IFRC (2026-10-28) ---
DESCRIPTION OF THE EVENT
Hostilities in Yemen in July 2026 intensified further in late August and early September, triggering large-scale population movements and increased humanitarian needs across affected governorates. Families have fled affected areas toward neighbouring governorates, and cross-border movements to Djibouti have added a regional dimension to the crisis.
According to IOM’s Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), 251,125 people, representing 41,853 households, were displaced between 1 August and 17 October 2026. Broader IOM tracking recorded 271,020 displaced people, representing 45,170 households, between 1 January and 17 October 2026. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates in Sana’a has also reported approximately 80,000 displaced households. However, these Ministry figures have not been independently verified and differ in geographic coverage and methodology from IOM data. They should therefore be presented separately. The IOM figures represent recorded displacement movements during specific reporting periods, not Yemen’s total internally displaced population.
This escalation compounds an already protracted humanitarian crisis. The IFRC Emergency Appeal identifies 22.3 million people requiring humanitarian assistance nationwide, with approximately 5.2 million people already internally displaced before the latest escalation. Years of escalation, economic deterioration, food insecurity, recurrent disease outbreaks and weakened public services have reduced households’ capacity to withstand further shocks.
Fighting and insecurity are disrupting access to healthcare, education, markets and essential supplies. Displaced families arriving with limited possessions require shelter, household items, food, safe water and healthcare, while host communities face increasing pressure on services and resources. Repeated displacement also further erodes livelihoods and increases protection risks. This volatile situation then creates the potential for further movement and underscores the need for sustained humanitarian access and a flexible response.
Severity of humanitarian conditions
Impact on accessibility, availability, quality, use and awareness of goods and services
Displaced families face overlapping barriers to shelter, food, safe water, sanitation, education and healthcare. Insecurity and road closures disrupt markets, supply routes and humanitarian assistance, while lost livelihoods, depleted assets and rising prices reduce households’ purchasing power. Isolated communities face particularly severe constraints as commercial supplies and essential services become increasingly difficult to sustain.
An Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) analysis released in May projected that approximately 5.4 million people in Government-controlled areas would face IPC Phase 3 or above during October–December 2026. However, this geographically limited projection predates the latest escalation and does not capture its full consequences.
Shelter and sanitation conditions remain critical as well and IOM assessments in displacement sites on 5 October identified inadequate safe shelter as the most significant vulnerability. Some households were sleeping outdoors, while women and girls highlighted shortages of appropriate latrines, compromising privacy, dignity and safety. On 14 October, the World Food Programme (WFP) reported that 800,000 people were beyond its reach.
Health and education disruptions also further restrict essential support. UNICEF has reported interrupted healthcare and schooling as well as damage to infrastructure serving children and families. Displacement also increases the need for accessible information on available assistance, functioning facilities and referral options.
Impact on physical and mental well-being
Violence, inadequate shelter and interrupted healthcare increase risks of injury, untreated illness and preventable deaths, and since the onset of this latest escalation, ICRC has highlighted that rising casualties and displacement are straining Yemen’s already-weakened health system. Demand for emergency care in turn reduces capacity for other services, while displaced people struggle to access medicines, vaccination, maternal healthcare and treatment for chronic disease.
Cholera in particular presents an immediate public health concern. UN News reported on 13 October that Yemen had recorded 8,034 suspected cholera cases and 27 deaths in 2026, with one-quarter of suspected cases occurring in the preceding four weeks. Disrupted services, overcrowded shelters and inadequate access to safe water increase the risk of cholera transmission. Poor sanitation and hygiene conditions in informal settlements then further heighten outbreak risks, while delayed treatment increases the danger of severe dehydration and death.
Food insecurity further compounds these risks against a backdrop of severe pre-existing nutritional vulnerabilities, with WFP reporting 2.2 million acutely malnourished children under five nationwide. Continued isolation and interrupted assistance threaten further deterioration, particularly among young children and pregnant and lactating women. Fear, bereavement, separation and repeated displacement also increase psychological distress and psychosocial support needs.
Risks and vulnerabilities
Repeated displacement is further exhausting households' coping capacities. A rapid assessment conducted by IOM across three displacement sites found that approximately 45 per cent of assessed households had experienced previous displacement. Associated asset losses and unstable livelihoods further undermine their ability to cope, increasing the risk of harmful coping strategies, including child labour. These findings reflect the households assessed in the three sites and should not be generalized to the wider displaced population.
Female-headed households, separated children, older people, persons with disabilities and people requiring regular treatment face overlapping barriers to safe movement and assistance in this context as well. Women and girls face additional protection concerns too where shelter and sanitation are inadequate, while people with limited mobility or resources may be unable to leave isolated areas or reach services.
Internal displacement and cross-border movements thus create distinct but intersecting needs. WFP’s 16 October reporting described families travelling for days without adequate food or water, as well as inordinate pressure on reception arrangements in Djibouti. These conditions compound the vulnerabilities of displaced populations while placing additional strain on host communities, shared services and already limited resources.
Assistance should therefore prioritize vulnerability across groups, combining accessible services, protection referrals, reliable information and community feedback. The evidence indicates worsening hunger and disease risks but does not establish a confirmed famine classification.
